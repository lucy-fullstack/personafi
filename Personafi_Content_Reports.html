06

Norm's voice, in five patterns

Pulled directly from the posts that performed best. This is the brief the 20 new posts are written against — not a generic LinkedIn "authentic voice," Norm's specific one.

The reframe sentence

A short, quotable line that flips a common assumption. It usually lands mid-post and gets echoed in the comments.

"Being needed felt good. Being useful meant I could disappear for a day and everything still ran fine."
Scene, not summary

His highest performers describe one specific moment — a hospital room, a coffee shop, a Tuesday — rather than a general opinion.

"When you're sitting in a hospital room waiting for test results, you stop caring about the email you forgot to send."
Short paragraphs, one idea each

Almost never more than two sentences per paragraph. It reads fast on mobile and gives every line room to land on its own.

Question, not CTA

Every post ends by asking the reader something specific — never "book a call," never a link. The comments are where the credibility compounds.

"What's one thing you've done as a leader that helped your team take more ownership?"
Proof, never pitch

Personafi, clients and the Colombia/Barranquilla team show up as texture inside a real story — never as a feature list or an offer.

Sparse emoji, high signal

One emoji, maybe, when it's doing real work (🎗️ for the survivor posts, 🇨🇴 for the Colombia team) — never as decoration.

Olga's voice, in six patterns

Pulled directly from the posts that performed best. Distinct from Norm's voice on purpose — this is Olga's specific register, not a shared house style.

The concept reframe

She regularly redefines a common term by naming what was wrong with it first.

"'Remote' framed location as the defining feature of the work. It isn't. Once I started thinking of it as 'management that happens to be remote,' the priorities shifted."
A framework, honestly earned

DSA (Drive, Satisfaction, Alignment), DMAIC applied to hiring, three meeting types — her Lean Six Sigma / Scrum background shows up as structure, not jargon.

One person, one turnaround

Her two best-performing posts each follow one anonymized team member's specific arc — not a general observation about people.

"Three months ago, a team member got promoted to a leadership role. She had the skills but not the confidence... little by little, she became the leader she already had the potential to be."
Short lines, arrow lists

She uses → to break an idea into sub-points more often than Norm's plain paragraph style — a visible trace of process-mapping habits.

Closes with an insight, or a challenge

Not every post ends in a question. Several end on a standalone aphorism, and a few end with a direct, doable challenge for the reader that week.

"This week, audit your recurring meetings. Ask one question: if this got cancelled, would anyone notice?"
Credentials worn lightly

Lean, Six Sigma, DMAIC and Scrum get named directly, anchoring soft people-lessons to a hard methodology rather than hiding the expertise.

Format check: short vs. long, photo vs. none, people vs. objects

Average engagement rate by format, across every post we have both a screenshot and a matched analytics figure for (45 posts across all four profiles) — this answers the standing question directly.

People, clearly. A real photo of a real person — a team member, a founder, a client moment — averages more than double the engagement rate of a plain text post or a designed graphic (4.3% vs. 2.0% and 2.0%). That holds even if you drop Norm's 14.4% outlier: people-photos still average 3.3%. Designed "Now Hiring" graphics and screenshot-style images performed no better than plain text — a real photo of a person is doing real work that a graphic isn't. Length didn't show a clean pattern on its own — the best post of the cycle was long (Norm's letter), the second-best was medium (Tania's), and several long, framework-style posts landed at the bottom. Length looks like it's riding on specificity rather than driving anything itself: a long post about one real person holds attention; a long post explaining a general system doesn't. And reach isn't the same signal as engagement — the single highest-reach post of the whole cycle (Jessica's tips post, 2,864 impressions) had no image at all and converted at just 0.38%, proof that a post can travel far without a photo, but won't make anyone stop scrolling without one.

06

What's next: month 2

This was the first month of content, from a standing start on three of four profiles. Here's what month 2 should aim for, and what needs to change to get there.

1

Close the attribution gap for Norm, Olga and Tania

Set up a simple way to catch a LinkedIn-sourced lead if one arrives: a "how did you hear about us" step on new inbound inquiries, and a request that all three flag any comment, DM or call that references a specific post. Without this, month 3's report can't say anything more definitive about client impact than month 1's could.

2

Give people an explicit reason to follow, on all four accounts

None of the 80 posts this cycle asked for the follow, and none of the four profiles gained a single net follower despite real reach growth. Month 2 should test a direct, low-friction ask tied to a named, recognizable series (e.g. "follow for more" on a numbered pillar), on at least one post per person per week.

3

Keep at or above 20 CVs for Jessica

The only fully confirmed funnel result this cycle. Month 2's target should be explicit — matching this cycle's 20, or beating it — and should test her weekend-engagement finding with at least one Saturday post.

4

Plan the back half of October

Each person's 20 posts run Sep 21–Oct 16 — four weeks. If daily weekday posting continues through end of October, each profile needs a second batch of roughly 15 more posts, built the same way: pull that window's real analytics, re-run the pillar analysis, expand from whatever wins.

5

Re-run this same analysis after batch one finishes

Once the Sep 21–Oct 16 posts have run, pull each person's LinkedIn analytics for that exact window (screenshots plus the native export, same as this cycle) so month 2 gets graded by the same real-numbers standard as month 1 — including whether the personal-story pillar held up a second time, or whether it was a one-cycle novelty effect.
